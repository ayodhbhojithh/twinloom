Two hosting models

Hosting can sit with us or with you. Which one applies changes who holds the account, who is billed and who carries the day-to-day operational duties.

Managed hosting

This is the default where we provide an ongoing care plan.

  • The website and its content remain your asset.
  • The hosting account is held and operated by us.
  • We manage deployment, updates, monitoring, backups and recovery covered by the plan.
  • You do not need infrastructure access for routine publishing or commerce.
  • The agreement contains an exit and migration process.

This is managed ownership, not complete operational handover.

Client-controlled hosting

This may suit a business with its own technical team, group policy or existing cloud environment.

  • The hosting account and supplier relationship sit with you.
  • We receive the access needed to deploy and support the site.
  • You control billing, account security and changes made by other administrators.
  • Our ability to meet support targets depends on the environment remaining within the agreed configuration.
  • The proposal states who is responsible for backups, monitoring and supplier support.

The more parties with infrastructure access, the more carefully change responsibility must be managed.

Separate the jobs that change at different speeds

The public website, the content editor, the shop and the business systems do different jobs.

Treating them as one undifferentiated product can look simpler at the beginning, but it often makes performance, security, ownership and later change harder.

Our default is to separate responsibilities and connect them through documented interfaces.

The public website

It is responsible for:

  • visual design;
  • responsive behaviour;
  • performance;
  • forms and interactions;
  • presenting content;
  • connection to commerce or other systems;
  • metadata and structured information;
  • accessibility behaviour;
  • analytics and consent implementation.

That may include:

  • locations;
  • resources;

It does not automatically control the whole visual layout. The public site applies the agreed design, which helps keep published pages consistent and prevents an editor from accidentally dismantling the interface.

WordPress is often the default recommendation for regular publishing. Other systems—or no separate system—can be better in some cases.

The real question is who needs to publish, how often and what happens when you leave

A blog does not have to live inside the same software that renders the public website.

The public site is designed around customers. The publishing system is used by the people creating and maintaining content. They can be separate systems connected in a deliberate way.

There are three practical options.

Option 1 — WordPress as the publishing system

Our usual recommendation for regular publishing

Your team writes in WordPress. The public website reads the approved content and presents it in the site’s own design.

Advantages

  • familiar to many editors;
  • scheduling, revisions, categories and multiple users;
  • a large pool of people who can support it;
  • established import and export routes;
  • no need to ask a developer for every article;
  • suitable for articles and structured repeatable content when configured properly.

Costs and responsibilities

  • a separate hosting or service cost;
  • updates and security maintenance;
  • user and plugin discipline;
  • configuration and integration work;
  • ongoing support under the care plan.

Good fit when

  • the business publishes regularly;
  • several people may contribute;
  • portability and broad support matter;
  • the team already knows WordPress;
  • a marketing partner expects to work in it.

WordPress holds the content. It does not need to dictate the public design or become the customer-facing runtime.

Option 2 — A hosted content system

Examples include managed “headless” content platforms and publishing services.

Advantages

  • infrastructure and core updates are handled by the supplier;
  • editorial interfaces can be clean and focused;
  • content models can be tailored;
  • APIs are designed for separated public websites;
  • less system maintenance for us to perform.

Costs and responsibilities

  • licence pricing may depend on users, usage or features;
  • the editor may require custom configuration;
  • fewer generalist agencies may know the exact setup;
  • export quality and portability vary;
  • the client depends on the supplier’s product and pricing decisions.

Good fit when

  • the team wants a tightly controlled editor;
  • the organisation already uses the platform;
  • the content model is more important than familiar WordPress workflows;
  • the licence and exit route have been checked.

We compare current supplier pricing and export options at quotation time. A static article should not publish prices that can change without notice.

Option 3 — No separate publishing system

You send approved content to us and we publish it under the care plan or as ad hoc work.

Advantages

  • no separate platform or training;
  • no additional account to manage;
  • appropriate for infrequent updates;
  • content can be kept in ordinary business documents.

Costs and responsibilities

  • publishing depends on our availability and service terms;
  • every change goes through another person;
  • less suitable for time-sensitive or frequent content;
  • the business needs its own organised source archive.

Good fit when

  • there are only a few articles each year;
  • one person approves everything;
  • paying for a publishing system would create more administration than value.

Buying a system for four annual updates can be unnecessary.

What happens after launch

Before launch, the website has been reviewed by people who already know the business and the project.

After launch, real visitors arrive with different devices, questions, patience and expectations. The first weeks are for observing, correcting and learning—not immediately redesigning everything.

The first day

We confirm:

  • the domain resolves correctly;
  • HTTPS is active;
  • key pages load;
  • forms and transactions work;
  • redirects are behaving;
  • analytics is receiving appropriate data;
  • indexing controls are correct;
  • monitoring is active;
  • the client can access the systems it needs.

Where a launch replaces an existing website, the old environment should not be destroyed until the rollback and retention position is clear.

The first week

We watch for:

  • failed forms or email delivery;
  • broken redirects;
  • missing assets;
  • unexpected mobile issues;
  • error logs;
  • third-party connection problems;
  • search crawling and sitemap processing;
  • misleading or incomplete content;
  • support questions from the client team.

Defects in the agreed build are handled under the post-launch period. New ideas are recorded and prioritised rather than mixed into emergency fixes.

The first month

The first review should consider:

  • which pages people use;
  • whether key actions are completed;
  • where visitors leave important journeys;
  • enquiry quality;
  • common questions not answered;
  • content the team finds difficult to maintain;
  • search impressions and indexing;
  • performance under real use;
  • operational effort created or removed.

A low-traffic site may not have enough data for confident design conclusions after one month. Observation should be proportional to the evidence.

Search after launch

Submitting a sitemap and making pages indexable does not create an immediate ranking.

For a replaced site, we monitor old and new URLs, redirects, errors and important search pages. For a new site, we establish whether pages are being discovered and what demand begins to appear.

Ongoing visibility requires useful content, evidence, consistency and—where competition justifies it—continued search work.

Content after launch

The website should change when the business changes.

Review:

  • services;
  • prices and offers;
  • people and responsibilities;
  • case studies;
  • opening hours and locations;
  • policies;
  • contact routes;
  • screenshots and product information;
  • outdated claims.

A content owner inside the business is as important as a technical care plan.

Deciding what to improve

Use this order:

  1. Repair anything blocking a customer.
  2. Correct inaccurate or risky content.
  3. Improve important journeys with clear evidence of friction.
  4. Strengthen pages with commercial demand.
  5. Add content or features with a defined purpose.
  6. Leave low-value cosmetic ideas until the higher-value work is done.

The fact that a feature is possible is not a reason to build it.

AI visibility: what can actually be done

There is no universal submission form that guarantees a business will be mentioned, cited or recommended by an AI assistant.

Different products use different combinations of web crawling, search indexes, licensed sources, product data and model behaviour. These arrangements change.

The sensible goal is to make accurate public information accessible, explicit and well supported, then measure the referrals and outcomes that can be observed.

1. Decide what can be accessed

A website can publish instructions for recognised crawlers and indexing systems.

The business should decide separately:

  • whether public pages may be indexed for search;
  • whether recognised AI search crawlers may access them;
  • whether recognised model-training crawlers may access them;
  • which private, draft or customer pages must never be public;
  • whether a page should be excluded from indexing entirely.

These controls depend on the crawler respecting them. They are not access control for confidential information.

2. Make the business explicit

A system should not need to infer basic facts from a slogan.

Important pages should state:

  • the business name;
  • what it provides;
  • who it serves;
  • relevant locations;
  • key people and credentials;
  • the relationship between trading and legal names;
  • products or services;
  • contact and policy information;
  • dates where freshness matters.

This also improves clarity for human visitors.

3. Publish evidence worth using

Original evidence is more useful than generic claims.

Examples include:

  • detailed case studies;
  • named authors with relevant experience;
  • original research or data;
  • clear methods;
  • product specifications;
  • transparent pricing;
  • dated policies;
  • real answers to customer questions;
  • comparison criteria;
  • references to authoritative sources.

Producing hundreds of interchangeable articles does not create evidence.

4. Use structured information accurately

Structured data can give eligible systems explicit clues about the type and meaning of visible content.

Use it where a supported vocabulary accurately describes the page. Do not add hidden claims, misleading reviews or markup for content the visitor cannot see.

Structured data can create eligibility or understanding. It does not guarantee display.

5. Build consistency beyond the website

The business name, address, services, profiles and important facts should be consistent across relevant trusted sources.

Depending on the business, that may include:

  • business profiles;
  • professional directories;
  • trade bodies;
  • marketplace listings;
  • media coverage;
  • partner websites;
  • product feeds;
  • social profiles.

This is not an invitation to create hundreds of low-quality listings.

6. Measure what is observable

Where platforms expose referral information, track:

  • visits;
  • landing pages;
  • actions completed;
  • enquiries;
  • assisted conversions;
  • questions that appear to trigger discovery.

Also monitor brand searches, citations and customer-reported discovery carefully. The data will remain incomplete.

7. Keep the promise honest

None of the six steps buys an answer generated by somebody else’s system. What we will and will not commit to is written out on the service page rather than here, so there is one version of it.
